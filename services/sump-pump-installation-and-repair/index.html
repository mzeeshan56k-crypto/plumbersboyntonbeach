Warning signs

Signs Your Sump Pump Is About to Fail

Pumps rarely die silently. Catch these and you avoid the flood.

  • The pump runs constantly, even in dry weather
  • It cycles on and off rapidly in short bursts
  • Grinding, rattling or unusually loud operation
  • Visible rust or corrosion on the pump body
  • Water in the pit that is not being pumped out
  • The pump does not start when the float is lifted
  • Standing water around the pit or discharge point
  • A unit older than seven years with no service history

Test yours before every storm season. Pour a bucket of water into the pit. If the pump does not start and clear it quickly, call.

Backup systems

Why Battery Backup Matters for Sump Pumps in Hurricane Country

A primary pump on household power solves the wrong half of the problem in South Florida, because our heaviest rain usually arrives with an outage attached.

The scenario to plan for

Eight inches of rain in a day, a saturated water table already at the surface, and the power out for hours. The pit fills, the pump has no electricity, and water enters the structure. Battery backup exists for precisely this window.

Battery backup pumps

A secondary DC pump with a deep-cycle battery and a maintained charger runs when utility power fails. Runtime depends on battery capacity and cycle frequency, and we size it to your pit and typical inflow rather than a generic figure.

Water-powered backups

Where municipal water pressure is available, a water-powered ejector needs no battery and no maintenance charge cycle. It uses potable water to move pit water, and it requires a backflow preventer, so it must be installed correctly.

Alarms and monitoring

A high-water alarm is inexpensive and tells you the primary pump has stopped keeping up. Wi-Fi monitoring is worth considering for seasonal residents who are not in the home when a storm hits.

Test before June

Pour a bucket of water into the pit at the start of hurricane season. If the pump does not start and clear it promptly, deal with it before the first storm rather than during it.

Boynton Beach conditions

Water Table and Storm Season in Boynton Beach

Boynton Beach sits low and flat, with groundwater often only a few feet below the surface. In the June through October wet season, sustained rain saturates the sandy soil quickly and the water table climbs. That is when pits fill, pumps run hard, and weak units burn out.

Hurricane season compounds the risk. The same storm that dumps eight inches of rain also knocks out power, which is why a pump without battery backup is only half a solution here. We install backup systems on most residential jobs for this reason.

Discharge routing is the other local detail. Water pumped out has to go somewhere that will not simply drain back toward the house through saturated soil. We route discharge lines well away from the foundation and check that the outlet is not going to be underwater during a heavy rain event.

Drainage

Discharge Routing and Yard Drainage That Keeps Water Away

A working pump that discharges badly simply recirculates water. Where the water goes matters as much as whether the pump runs.

Distance from the foundation

Discharge should terminate well away from the structure, not a few feet from the wall where saturated sandy soil lets it drain straight back toward the pit. Short discharge lines are the most common installation shortcut we correct.

Outlet elevation

The termination point must stay above standing water during heavy rain. An outlet that ends up submerged cannot discharge, which stalls the pump exactly when it is needed most.

Check valve and freeze-free fittings

A functioning check valve stops discharged water from draining back into the pit each cycle, which otherwise doubles run time and shortens pump life considerably.

Grading and gutters

Much of what reaches a pit arrives from the surface. Downspouts extended away from the house and soil graded to fall away from the foundation reduce inflow substantially, and both are cheaper than a larger pump.

Sealed pit lids

A sealed lid limits humidity, odor and debris entering the pit, and it keeps the float from being jammed by anything falling in, which is a surprisingly common cause of failure.

Before you call

How to Test a Sump Pump Yourself

A sump pump that has not run in months is exactly the one that fails during a storm. Test it before the season.

  • Pour a bucket of water into the pit and watch the float lift and the pump start.
  • Confirm the pump shuts off cleanly when the level drops.
  • Check the discharge outside is actually running and is not blocked.
  • Look for debris in the pit that could jam the float.

One thing not to do: If the pump runs but the level does not drop, stop and call. That usually means the check valve or the discharge line is blocked, and running it dry damages the motor.

Urgency

When Is a Sump Pump Failure an Emergency?

A pump that tests fine can wait for a scheduled service. Call immediately if:

  • Water is rising in the pit and the pump is not running.
  • The pump runs continuously and never shuts off.
  • There is standing water near electrical equipment.
  • Heavy rain is forecast and the pump has already failed a test.

If any of those describe what you are seeing, call (833) 567-2788 now rather than booking online. Emergency calls are moved to the front of the queue.

FAQs

Sump Pump FAQs

How long does a sump pump last?

Seven to ten years with normal use. Pumps that run frequently in high water table areas wear faster.

Do I really need a battery backup in Florida?

Yes. Our heaviest rain often comes with power outages. Without backup, the pump is offline exactly when it is needed.

How often should the pump be tested?

Twice a year, and always before the start of hurricane season in June.

Can you install a pump where there is no pit?

Yes, we can excavate and set a pit where drainage conditions call for one, then route the discharge properly.

Why does my pump run all the time?

Usually a stuck float, a failed check valve letting water flow back in, or a genuinely high water table. We test all three.

Is battery backup actually worth it here?

In storm season, yes. The conditions that make the pump essential are the same conditions that take the power out, and a primary pump without backup is offline exactly when it is needed. A backup unit also covers you if the primary fails mechanically.
