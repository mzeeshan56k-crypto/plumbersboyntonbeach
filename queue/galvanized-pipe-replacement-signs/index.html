Galvanized Pipe: The Signs It Needs Replacing

It closes from the inside. Pressure is the symptom you notice.

Galvanized supply pipe is still in service in some of the oldest homes around Boynton Beach and the historic districts nearby. It is steel pipe with a zinc coating, and once that coating is gone the steel corrodes and the inside diameter starts closing up.

Unlike copper, galvanized rarely fails with a dramatic burst. It fails by slowly strangling itself, so the complaint is almost always pressure rather than leaks.

The signs

  • Water pressure that has fallen gradually over years rather than suddenly.
  • Rust colored water, especially after the house has sat unused.
  • Hot side noticeably worse than cold, because heat accelerates the corrosion.
  • Visible threaded steel pipe at the water heater or under the house.
  • Flow that drops sharply when a second fixture opens.

Pressure that fell suddenly is a different problem. A failing regulator does that, and it is a much cheaper fix.

Why patching does not work

Cutting out one bad section and threading in new steel leaves the rest of the system just as narrow. Worse, disturbing old galvanized often breaks loose scale that then blocks fixtures downstream.

That is why galvanized is usually a repipe conversation rather than a repair conversation. What a repipe involves sets the expectations.

What replaces it

PEX and CPVC are both common choices, and copper is still used where the owner prefers it. The right answer depends on routing and access more than on the material itself.

Comparing the three materials covers the differences that actually matter in this climate.

Why galvanized pipe closes up from the inside

Galvanized steel pipe is steel with a zinc coating. The zinc protects the steel for as long as it lasts, and in water it slowly goes. Once it is gone the steel is exposed, and it corrodes. The corrosion product is rust, which occupies several times the volume of the steel it came from, so it does not wash away. It builds inward.

That is the central fact about galvanized pipe and the reason it behaves unlike any other plumbing material. It does not usually leak first. It narrows first. A three quarter inch supply line can close down to a fraction of its original bore while remaining perfectly watertight, and the homeowner experiences falling pressure rather than a plumbing failure.

The hot side goes first, because heat accelerates everything. Horizontal runs collect more deposit than vertical ones. And the narrowing is worst at threaded joints, where the pipe wall is thinnest and the fitting creates turbulence.

Confirming what you have

  1. Look at exposed pipe where it enters the house or near the water heater. Galvanized is dull gray steel with threaded fittings, and a magnet sticks to it.
  2. Check whether a magnet sticks. Copper and plastic are not magnetic, which settles the question in seconds.
  3. Tap it. Steel pipe has a solid ring that plastic does not.
  4. Find the age of the house. Galvanized supply was standard into roughly the 1960s and appears in later work occasionally.
  5. Check whether anything was partially replaced. Mixed systems are extremely common, with a visible section of copper hiding galvanized runs behind the walls.
  6. Ask a plumber to cut and show you a section if a repipe is being considered. A cross-section of narrowed pipe makes the decision obvious in a way that any description cannot.

A section of copper at the water heater does not mean the house was repiped. Replacing the visible pipe and leaving the concealed runs was a common shortcut, and the narrow sections are precisely the ones nobody can see.

How to tell it from the other causes of low pressure

Low pressure differential diagnosis

  • Onset. Galvanized narrowing: Gradual over years. Other cause: Sudden points at a regulator or a valve.
  • Hot versus cold. Galvanized narrowing: Hot noticeably worse. Other cause: Equal points at supply or regulator.
  • Affected fixtures. Galvanized narrowing: Worse at the far end of the house. Other cause: One fixture only points at an aerator or a stop.
  • Rusty water. Galvanized narrowing: Common, worse after the house sits unused. Other cause: Clear water points elsewhere.
  • Static pressure at a hose bib. Galvanized narrowing: Normal at the bib, poor inside. Other cause: Low at the bib points upstream.
  • Flow with two fixtures open. Galvanized narrowing: Falls sharply. Other cause: Holds up if the pipe is clear.

The hose bib test is the most telling of these. An outdoor bib often taps off close to where water enters the house, so good pressure there with poor flow at an upstairs shower points directly at what is between the two.

Why partial replacement disappoints

Replacing the accessible galvanized runs and leaving the concealed ones is the most common way homeowners spend money on this problem without solving it. Flow through a series of pipes is limited by the narrowest section, so leaving one closed-up run in place caps the improvement at whatever that run allows.

There is also a corrosion consideration. Joining copper directly to galvanized steel accelerates corrosion at the connection through galvanic action, which is why a dielectric union belongs at any such joint. Mixed systems assembled without them develop leaks exactly where the two metals meet.

The sequence that does work is replacing a complete path, from the point of entry through to the fixtures, even if you do that one branch at a time. Our repiping work is often staged that way, and staging it properly from the start is what makes each phase deliver an improvement you can feel.

Frequently asked questions

How old does galvanized pipe have to be to fail?

Most galvanized supply installed before 1960 is at or past the end of its useful life. Forty to seventy years is the usual range and warm damp conditions shorten it.

Is rusty water from galvanized pipe dangerous?

It is unpleasant rather than acutely harmful, but it indicates advanced internal corrosion and it stains fixtures and laundry.

Can I replace just the hot side?

You can, and some owners do as a staged approach. It helps, but the cold side will follow on the same timeline.

How do I know what I have?

Look at the pipe at the water heater and under a sink. Or call our Boynton Beach plumbers on (833) 567-2788 and we will identify it.

How long does galvanized pipe last?

Commonly forty to seventy years before flow becomes a problem, with a wide range depending on water chemistry and how much hot water ran through it. In practice almost all galvanized supply piping still in service is past the point where narrowing has started, since very little of it was installed after the 1960s.

Is the rusty water from galvanized pipe harmful?

It is unappealing rather than dangerous at the levels normally involved, and iron is not a health contaminant in the way lead is. The real concern is what the discoloration indicates about the condition of the pipe. Water that runs brown after the house has sat unused for a few days is telling you how much deposit is sitting in the line.

Can galvanized pipe be cleaned out instead of replaced?

There is no reliable way to do it on a supply line. The deposit is bonded rust that has consumed part of the pipe wall, so removing it aggressively would mean removing what is left of the pipe. Drain lines can sometimes be descaled because they are not pressurized and are much larger. Supply pipe gets replaced.
