The Plumbing Checklist for a Bathroom Remodel

Every expensive remodel mistake was decided before the tile went on.

Bathroom remodel plumbing in a Boynton Beach home runs in two stages. Rough-in happens while the walls are open, and trim happens once the tile and cabinets are set. Almost every costly mistake in a remodel is made at the first stage and discovered at the second.

The fix is a short checklist settled before anyone opens a wall. Fixture positions, valve depths, drain slope and venting. Get those agreed and the trim visit becomes routine.

Settle these before rough-in

  1. Confirm whether any fixture is moving location. That changes drains and venting, not just supply.
  2. Choose the fixtures first. Valve depth depends on the finished wall thickness.
  3. Check what is below the bathroom. A second story relocation is a different job.
  4. Agree who supplies what and confirm the fixtures are on site before trim day.
  5. Confirm the drain has enough fall for the new layout.
  6. Agree where access panels will go.

Do not let tile go on before the plumbing inspection. Opening a finished wall to correct a valve is the most expensive mistake in a remodel.

What the inspection is checking

Rough-in inspection points

  • Drain slope. Why it matters: Too little and waste sits. Too much and solids are left behind.
  • Venting. Why it matters: Without it, traps siphon and the room smells.
  • Valve depth. Why it matters: Trim will not seat correctly if it is wrong.
  • Pressure test. Why it matters: Finds a weeping joint before it is behind tile.

If the walls are open anyway, it is also the cheapest moment to replace old supply pipe. A repipe during a remodel avoids paying twice for the same drywall.

Permits and sequencing

Moving drains, changing in wall valves or altering venting needs a permit. A like for like fixture swap usually does not.

Sequencing matters as much as the work. Rough-in, inspect, close, tile, then trim. Skipping the inspection to keep the tiler on schedule is a false economy.

Two jobs are worth folding into the same project. Shower valve replacement and a failing closet flange are both far cheaper while the room is already open.

Why plumbing decisions have to come first

In a bathroom remodel the plumbing is the least visible and the least reversible part of the work, and it has to be right before anything is closed up. Once the walls are boarded and the floor is tiled, moving a drain by six inches means undoing finished work.

That is why the plumbing rough-in sits early in the sequence and why the fixture choices have to be made before it. A plumber roughing in cannot place a drain or set a valve depth without knowing which toilet, which tub and which shower system are going in. Choosing fixtures after rough-in is the single most common cause of rework in bathroom projects.

It also affects cost in a way that is easy to underestimate. Keeping fixtures in their existing positions is dramatically cheaper than relocating them, particularly on a slab where a drain move means breaking concrete. A layout change that looks minor on a drawing can be the largest line item in the project.

The decisions to lock down before rough-in

  1. Exact fixture models for the toilet, sink, tub and shower, with their rough-in dimensions available to the plumber.
  2. The shower valve system, since rough-in depth depends on the finished wall thickness.
  3. Whether the shower is a pan or a tiled base, because drain type and height differ.
  4. Shower head, body spray and handheld positions, marked on the wall at the height you actually want them.
  5. Niche and bench locations, so they are not fighting a pipe or a vent.
  6. Vanity type and width, since a wall-hung vanity needs the drain and supplies at a specific height.
  7. Whether a freestanding tub is on the table, which changes the supply and waste routing entirely.
  8. Lighting and ventilation positions, which share the same ceiling space as vent piping.
  9. Whether an access panel will be provided for the shower valve.
  10. Flooring thickness, which determines the final toilet flange height.

Walk the room with the plumber and mark heights on the studs with a pencil. Fifteen minutes of that avoids the most common complaints after a remodel, which are a shower head too low and a valve in an awkward position.

Where remodels go over budget

Common overruns and whether they are avoidable

  • Drain relocation. Why it happens: Layout changed after rough-in. Avoidable: Yes, finalize layout first.
  • Old drain found in poor condition. Why it happens: Cast iron exposed during demolition. Avoidable: Partly, camera first.
  • Flange height after new flooring. Why it happens: Floor thickness not accounted for. Avoidable: Yes, plan the buildup.
  • Valve depth wrong for finished wall. Why it happens: Wall thickness changed late. Avoidable: Yes, confirm before rough-in.
  • Supply lines need replacing. Why it happens: Old pipe exposed and at end of life. Avoidable: Partly, inspect first.
  • Venting corrections required. Why it happens: Existing arrangement did not meet requirements. Avoidable: No, and it is better found now.

Two of those are worth treating as near-certainties in an older home. If the house is of the age where cast iron drains and original supply pipe are likely, having both looked at before demolition turns a surprise into a line item. A camera run on the drain is cheap at that stage and expensive to wish for later.

Sequencing with the rest of the trades

Plumbing appears three times in a bathroom project and the gaps between those visits are where scheduling goes wrong. The first visit is demolition and rough-in, before drywall. The second is after tile, setting the fixtures and trim. The third is the punch list, dealing with whatever does not seat properly.

Between the first and second, the inspection happens if the work is permitted, and nothing should be boarded over until it has. Between the second and third, give the silicone and any setting materials the time the manufacturer specifies before the shower is used, which is longer than most people allow.

Tell the plumber who the other trades are and how the schedule runs. A plumber who knows the tile goes down on a particular day can sequence the rough-in to suit it. One who is called on the day the tile is finished will be fitting it in around other work, and the punch list gets longer.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need a permit for a bathroom remodel?

If drains, venting or in wall valves change, yes. Replacing a faucet or toilet in place generally does not.

Can I move the toilet a few feet?

Often, but the drain and vent have to move with it, and what is below decides how hard that is.

When should the plumber come?

Twice. Once for rough-in while the walls are open and once for trim after tile and cabinets.

Can you work with my contractor?

Yes, and it works best that way. Call Plumbers Boynton Beach on (833) 567-2788 or see kitchen and bathroom plumbing.

Do I need a permit for a bathroom remodel?

Replacing fixtures in place is treated differently from moving drains, altering venting or changing the layout, and the latter generally requires a permit. Check with the Boynton Beach building department or the relevant authority for your address before work starts, because retroactive permitting after tile is down is considerably more disruptive than doing it in order.

Can I move the toilet a few feet?

Sometimes, and it depends on the drain. A toilet needs adequate fall to the existing line, and on a slab the drain has to be reworked in concrete. A short move along the same run is often feasible, while moving to the opposite wall is a major item. Ask before the layout is settled rather than after.

When should the plumber be booked?

At the planning stage, not when demolition starts. The plumber's input on what can be moved and what cannot shapes a realistic layout, and booking early secures the rough-in slot in the schedule. A plumber brought in late is working around decisions already made, which is where avoidable cost comes from.
