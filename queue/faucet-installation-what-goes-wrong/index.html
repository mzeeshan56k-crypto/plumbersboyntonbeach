What Goes Wrong With Faucet Installation

The faucet is rarely the problem. The shutoffs underneath usually are.

Faucet installation in a Boynton Beach home looks simple and usually is. The call-backs we see almost never involve the faucet itself. They involve the parts underneath that were left alone because they were still working on the day.

Angle stops and supply lines are the two that matter. Both are cheap, both are easy to replace while the cabinet is already open, and both are what turn a one hour job into a house wide shutdown when they fail later.

The mistakes that cause call-backs

  • Reusing old angle stops that are seized or weeping.
  • Reusing old braided supply lines rather than fitting new ones.
  • Not checking the hole configuration on the sink before buying.
  • Overtightening plastic nuts on the faucet body.
  • Forgetting the new drain assembly that most faucets expect.

Old shutoffs are the biggest of these. Why angle stops should be replaced covers what they cost you when they are skipped.

What to check before buying

Before you order a faucet

  • Hole count and spacing on the sink deck. Why: A three hole sink will not take a single hole faucet without a plate.
  • Whether a drain assembly is included. Why: Many are, some are not, and sizes vary.
  • Finish against existing trim. Why: Brushed and polished finishes vary between makers.
  • Reach and height against the sink. Why: A tall spout in a shallow sink splashes constantly.

In coastal homes, finish durability matters more than it does inland. Salt air pits ordinary trim faster than most people expect.

Doing it properly

Shut off, replace the stops, fit new braided lines, set the faucet, fit the drain, then check for leaks under pressure with the cabinet dry and lined with paper.

If a shutoff will not close, stop. Forcing a seized stop is how a simple job turns into a burst line and a wet kitchen.

The same logic applies to showers. Replacing a shower valve is harder to redo, and hard water is what blocks the aerators afterwards.

The part of the job nobody plans for

Swapping a faucet is simple in principle. In practice, the difficulty almost never lies in the new faucet. It lies in what is already under the sink. The old mounting nuts are often corroded into place on a threaded shank you cannot see and can barely reach, and the angle stops have not been turned since the house was built.

That is why a job estimated at thirty minutes becomes two hours. Freeing a seized mounting nut in the back of a cabinet with eight inches of clearance is the real work, and occasionally the honest answer is cutting it off. A basin wrench is the tool that makes it possible and the reason plumbers own one.

The practical implication is to plan for the stops and the supply lines as part of the job rather than as a surprise. If the stops are original, budget to replace them. Doing it while the cabinet is already open and the water is already off costs very little extra, and discovering a stop will not close after the faucet is already removed costs a great deal.

Checking compatibility before you buy

What to verify against your sink

  • Number of holes. Why it matters: A single-hole faucet on a three-hole sink needs a deck plate.
  • Hole spacing. Why it matters: Centerset and widespread are not interchangeable.
  • Deck thickness. Why it matters: A thick stone counter may exceed the shank length.
  • Spout reach and height. Why it matters: A tall spout over a shallow sink splashes constantly.
  • Drain and pop-up. Why it matters: Some faucets include a drain assembly, some do not.
  • Supply connections. Why it matters: Integrated hoses versus separate risers changes what you need.
  • Sprayer type. Why it matters: A pull-down needs clearance below for the hose and weight.

Spout reach is the one that causes the most regret, because it is the one you cannot judge from a photograph. A spout that does not reach the center of the bowl makes the sink unpleasant to use every day, and it is not a fault you can fix afterwards.

Doing it so it does not leak

  1. Close both angle stops and open the old faucet to confirm the water is actually off.
  2. Clear the cabinet completely and put a towel and a light in there. Working blind is where mistakes happen.
  3. Disconnect the supplies, then the mounting nuts, then lift the old faucet out. Expect debris and old sealant on the deck.
  4. Clean the deck fully. New gaskets seal against a clean surface, and old silicone or mineral crust stops them.
  5. Set the new faucet with the manufacturer's gasket. Add sealant only where the instructions call for it, since over-sealing can trap water.
  6. Tighten the mounting hardware evenly and check the faucet is square to the sink before the final tightening.
  7. Fit new supply lines rather than reusing the old ones, and do not overtighten the fittings.
  8. Open the stops slowly, then run both hot and cold with the aerator removed to flush debris, then refit the aerator and check every joint with a dry hand.

Flushing with the aerator out matters. Disturbing the lines dislodges sediment, and the aerator is the first place it lodges. Skipping this is why a new faucet sometimes has poor flow from day one.

When it drips a week later

A new faucet that develops a drip is almost always one of three things, and none of them means a faulty product. Debris from the installation has lodged in the cartridge and is holding it slightly open. The cartridge was damaged during installation by forcing a handle or overtightening. Or the supply connections are weeping rather than the faucet itself, and the water is tracking along the body.

Check the connections first with a dry paper towel on each joint, since that is the easiest to resolve and the easiest to misattribute. Then remove and flush the cartridge, which addresses the debris case. Only after both of those is the cartridge itself a reasonable suspect, and most manufacturers supply a replacement without argument.

High line pressure is the background factor worth checking if cartridges fail repeatedly. A faucet that goes through cartridges is often a house that is running at a pressure the fixture was never designed for, and no amount of cartridge replacement fixes that.

Frequently asked questions

Should I supply my own faucet?

Either works. If you buy it, check hole spacing and whether the drain assembly is included. If we supply it, the warranty is handled in one place.

How long does a faucet swap take?

Usually under an hour and a half per fixture when the shutoffs cooperate. Seized stops add time.

Why did my new faucet start dripping?

Usually a cartridge seated badly or debris from the line caught in it. Flushing the lines before fitting prevents most of these.

Can you handle several fixtures at once?

Yes, and it is more efficient that way. Call Plumbers Boynton Beach on (833) 567-2788 or see fixture installation.

Should I supply my own faucet?

It is perfectly normal and most plumbers will fit a fixture you provide. The trade-off is where responsibility sits if the unit is faulty, and that labor on a second visit is usually chargeable. Buying a fixture with parts that are readily available matters more than the brand, because the cartridge is what you will eventually need.

How long should a faucet swap take?

On accessible plumbing with stops that work, under an hour. On original stops and seized mounting hardware in a tight cabinet, two hours or more is realistic and is not a sign of anything going wrong. The honest version of the estimate includes the possibility of replacing the stops.

Can several fixtures be done in one visit?

Yes, and it is the economical way to do it. The water is off once, the tools come out once, and a plumber working through a list of faucets, stops and supply lines is far more efficient than three separate calls. If you have a handful of small items, group them.
