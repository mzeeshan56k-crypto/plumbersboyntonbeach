Plumbing Checks Before a Storm

The pump you never test is the one that fails during the storm.

Hurricane season plumbing preparation in Boynton Beach comes down to a few checks that take minutes and matter enormously when the weather turns. Storms bring two problems for plumbing. Power loss, which stops anything that needs electricity, and groundwater, which finds every weakness in a buried line.

Most of this list is free. The part that costs money is the part people skip, and it is usually the pump.

Before the season starts

  1. Test the sump pump by pouring a bucket of water into the pit and watching it run and shut off.
  2. Check the discharge line outside is clear and directs water away from the foundation.
  3. Confirm your main shutoff turns freely.
  4. Clear debris from yard drains and gutter outlets.
  5. Check for any drain that backs up after heavy rain, which points at a compromised line.

A line that backs up only after rain usually means groundwater entering through cracks. Roots and cracked joints are the common route in.

When a storm is forecast

  • Fill containers with drinking water in case supply is interrupted.
  • Fill a bathtub for flushing if you expect an extended outage.
  • Shut the main if you are evacuating, which prevents an unattended leak.
  • Turn the water heater to its vacation setting or off if you are leaving.
  • Photograph the property before the storm for insurance purposes.

If you are leaving the property empty, shutting off the main is the single most useful thing you can do.

After the storm

Check for sewer backups before using fixtures heavily. If the municipal system took on water, your line may be slow until it clears.

Watch the water heater and any pump that lost power. Check for leaks around equipment that sat in standing water, and do not restore power to a unit that was submerged.

If the property will be empty rather than occupied, the list changes. Leaving a Florida home empty covers that, and the lateral is what rain exposes.

Why storms affect plumbing at all

The connection is not obvious until you see what happens during and after a major storm here. Three separate mechanisms are at work, and each produces a different kind of call.

The first is the sanitary sewer system. Heavy, sustained rainfall raises groundwater and infiltrates sewer mains and laterals through cracks and open joints. A system carrying stormwater it was not designed for surcharges, and the backup finds the lowest connected opening, which is often a floor drain or a shower pan in a single-story home.

The second is loss of power. Lift stations in the municipal system depend on it, and so does any residential pump, including a well pump and a sewage ejector pump. A house that relies on a pump has no water or no drainage during an outage unless there is backup power.

The third is physical damage. Wind-driven debris breaks hose bibs and exposed pipe, flooding damages water heaters and pumps, and trees coming down take service lines and laterals with them.

Before the season

  1. Locate and exercise the main shutoff, and make sure every adult in the house can find and operate it in the dark.
  2. Have the drains and the lateral looked at if there is any history of slow drainage or backups after rain. A line that infiltrates groundwater is the one that will back up.
  3. Clear the roof vents of debris and nests, since a blocked vent makes drains sluggish exactly when you need them working.
  4. Check the water heater's pan and its discharge, and the straps if it sits high up or in an exposed position.
  5. Service any sump or ejector pump and test it by filling the basin rather than by assuming.
  6. Fit or verify a backwater valve if the property has a history of sewer backup, and have it checked rather than trusted.
  7. Replace old washing machine hoses and any supply line that is past its best, because a storm is a bad time for a connector to fail.
  8. Photograph the plumbing and the appliances with dates, which is useful if a claim follows.

The lateral inspection is the one with the clearest payoff. Backups after heavy rain are almost always infiltration, and infiltration is a defect that can be found and sealed outside of storm season.

When a storm is forecast

  • Fill containers for drinking water, and fill bathtubs for flushing. A bathtub holds enough to flush a toilet many times by pouring directly into the bowl.
  • Reduce water use during and immediately after the storm, which helps the sewer system and reduces your own backup risk.
  • Run the dishwasher and laundry before the storm rather than during it.
  • Shut the water off at the main if you are evacuating, and switch the water heater off or to its vacation setting.
  • Move anything valuable off the floor in areas with floor drains, which are where a sewer backup appears first.
  • Know where the sewer cleanout is, because relieving a backup outside is better than having it inside.
  • Charge whatever you need to operate a pump, and confirm any generator arrangement works before you need it.
  • Have the number of a plumber who answers after a storm, since demand will be high.

If the sewer backs up during a storm, stop using water in the house entirely. Every flush and every load of laundry adds to a system that has nowhere to put it.

After the storm

Work through it in a sensible order rather than all at once. Check for leaks first, including at every hose bib and any exposed pipe, since wind-driven debris breaks those most often. Then check that drains run freely, by filling and releasing a tub rather than running a tap.

If the property was flooded, treat the water heater and any pump as suspect. An appliance that has been submerged needs assessment rather than being switched back on, particularly gas equipment where controls and the burner assembly are affected. The same applies to any electrical component in a well system.

If water service was interrupted, follow whatever notice the utility issues about boiling or flushing before normal use. Run cold taps for several minutes to clear the lines of anything that entered during the loss of pressure, and change any point-of-use filter cartridges afterwards, since they will have caught whatever came through.

Frequently asked questions

Should I shut off the water before a hurricane?

If you are leaving, yes. If you are staying, no, because you will want water. Know where the valve is either way.

Why do drains back up after heavy rain?

Groundwater entering a cracked lateral, or the municipal system running at capacity. A line that only backs up after rain is worth inspecting.

Does a sump pump need a battery backup?

In storm season it is worth it. The conditions that make the pump essential are the same ones that take the power out.

Who do I call after a storm?

Call our Boynton Beach plumbers on (833) 567-2788. See sump pump repair if the pump failed.

Should I shut off the water before a hurricane?

If you are leaving the property, yes, without hesitation. It removes the possibility of an unattended failure while nobody is there, which is the worst category of loss. If you are staying, keep it on for sanitation and fill containers as well, and know where the valve is in case you need it quickly.

Why do drains back up after heavy rain?

Because groundwater is entering the sewer system through defects in the mains and in private laterals, filling pipes that were sized for sewage rather than stormwater. If your house backs up after rain and the neighbors do not, the defect is more likely to be in your lateral, and a camera inspection will find it.

Does a sump pump need a battery backup?

If it is protecting anything that matters, yes, because the storm that causes the water is the storm that takes the power. A battery backup or a water-powered secondary pump is the difference between a pump that works when needed and a pump that works when it is not needed. Test it annually rather than assuming.
