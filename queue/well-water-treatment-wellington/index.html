Treating Well Water in Western Palm Beach County

Well water varies house to house. Only a test tells you what to fit.

Well water treatment in Wellington and the western parts of Palm Beach County is a different problem from treating city water. There is no utility adding chlorine and no published quality report. What comes out of your tap depends on your own well and the ground around it.

That makes testing non negotiable. Two houses on the same road can need completely different equipment, and fitting a package system without a test is how owners end up treating the wrong thing.

What a well water test looks at

  • Hardness, which scales heaters and fixtures.
  • Iron, which stains fixtures and laundry a rust color.
  • Sulfur, which produces the rotten egg smell.
  • Sediment and turbidity, which clog valves and cartridges.
  • Bacteria, which is a separate and important question on any private well.

Iron and sulfur are the two that city water customers rarely deal with, and they need specific media rather than a general filter.

Typical equipment order

How a well system is usually staged

  • Sediment filtration. Purpose: Protects everything downstream from grit.
  • Iron or sulfur treatment. Purpose: Targets the specific contaminant found in the test.
  • Softener. Purpose: Handles hardness once the rest is removed.
  • Reverse osmosis at the kitchen. Purpose: Drinking water quality at one tap.

Order matters. Putting a softener first on iron heavy water fouls the resin and shortens its life considerably.

Pressure and the pump

Well systems bring their own pressure considerations, because a pump and tank replace the municipal supply. Treatment equipment adds resistance, so sizing has to account for the pressure you actually have.

If pressure is already marginal, treat that first. Causes of low pressure covers diagnosis, and softener or filter covers the equipment choice.

Well properties carry two extra obligations. Backflow testing on irrigation and the hardness question covered in hard water in Palm Beach County.

Why well water is a different problem

A municipal supply arrives treated, tested and with a published quality report. A private well arrives as whatever the aquifer contains, with no treatment and no testing unless the owner arranges it. That is the whole difference, and it is why equipment selection for a well property cannot start from assumptions.

In the western communities, including Wellington, Royal Palm Beach and the unincorporated areas, well supply is common. The shallow aquifer there typically carries significant hardness, frequently iron, often hydrogen sulfide producing a sulfur smell, and sometimes tannins from organic material which give the water a tea color.

Each of those needs different equipment, in a specific order, and the order is determined by the test results. This is the one situation in residential plumbing where buying equipment before testing is close to guaranteed to produce a poor outcome.

What to test for and why

Well water test items

  • Hardness. Why it matters: Scale on everything, soap performance. Equipment it points to: Softener.
  • Iron. Why it matters: Orange staining, fouls softener resin. Equipment it points to: Iron filter, before the softener.
  • Hydrogen sulfide. Why it matters: Rotten egg smell. Equipment it points to: Aeration or oxidizing filter.
  • pH. Why it matters: Low pH is corrosive to pipe and fittings. Equipment it points to: Neutralizing filter.
  • Tannins. Why it matters: Yellow or brown tint. Equipment it points to: Tannin filter or specialized resin.
  • Sediment and turbidity. Why it matters: Grit in fixtures, wears pumps and valves. Equipment it points to: Sediment filtration.
  • Bacteria, total coliform. Why it matters: Health matter, not aesthetic. Equipment it points to: Disinfection, and investigate the cause.
  • Nitrate. Why it matters: Health matter, particularly for infants. Equipment it points to: Reverse osmosis or specific treatment.

The last two are health items rather than nuisance items, and they are the reason an annual bacteriological test on a private well is sensible regardless of whether the water seems fine. Nothing about taste or appearance indicates bacterial contamination.

Getting the sequence right

  1. Test first, with a certified laboratory, and get numbers rather than impressions.
  2. Address pH before anything else if it is low, because corrosive water damages the equipment you are about to install as well as the plumbing.
  3. Remove sediment next, with filtration sized so it does not restrict flow.
  4. Deal with iron and sulfur before the softener, since both foul resin and shorten its life substantially.
  5. Soften after that, sized on the tested hardness and the household's actual water use.
  6. Add point-of-use treatment last, for drinking water specifically, once the whole-house problems are handled.
  7. Retest after the system is commissioned, at a tap, to confirm each stage is doing what it was bought to do.
  8. Retest annually thereafter, because well water changes with the water table, with rainfall and with time.

Equipment installed in the wrong order is the most common fault we find on well systems, and it usually shows up as a softener that stopped working years earlier because iron destroyed the resin.

The pump and pressure side

Treatment equipment adds resistance, and a well system has a finite amount of pressure to give. Every filter and every tank costs some of it, so a system designed without reference to the pump often produces water that is clean and disappointing to shower in.

The pressure tank is the other component to understand. It holds a cushion of air that smooths delivery and stops the pump cycling on and off constantly. A tank that has lost its air charge, or whose bladder has failed, makes the pump short cycle, and short cycling is what destroys pumps. Checking the air pressure annually is simple maintenance that extends the life of the most expensive component in the system.

When adding treatment, have the pump, the tank and the pressure switch settings looked at as part of the same job. A treatment system specified without that check can turn a working well into one that needs a new pump a year later.

Frequently asked questions

How often should well water be tested?

At least annually for bacteria and nitrates, and any time the taste, smell or color changes. A full panel is worth repeating every few years.

Why does my well water smell like rotten eggs?

That is usually sulfur, either from the water itself or from a reaction at the water heater anode. Testing tells you which, and the fixes are different.

Will a softener remove iron?

Only small amounts. Significant iron needs dedicated treatment ahead of the softener, or the resin fouls.

At least annually for bacteria and nitrate, and whenever anything changes in taste, smell or appearance. Testing is inexpensive relative to the equipment decisions it informs and the problems it rules out.

Why does my well water smell like rotten eggs?

Hydrogen sulfide, which in this region is often naturally present and sometimes produced by bacteria. One useful check is whether the smell is on the hot water only, which points at a reaction with the water heater's anode rod rather than the well. If it is on both hot and cold, it is in the supply and needs treatment at the point of entry.

Will a softener remove iron?

Only small amounts, and using it that way is a mistake. Iron fouls the resin, so a softener doing an iron filter's job gradually loses capacity and eventually stops softening at all. Where the test shows meaningful iron, a dedicated iron filter ahead of the softener protects the investment and actually solves the staining.
