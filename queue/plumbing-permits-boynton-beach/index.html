When Plumbing Work Needs a Permit

Water heaters, repipes, sewer and gas. Fixture swaps usually do not.

Plumbing permits in Boynton Beach apply to work that affects the safety of the system rather than to every repair. Water heater replacement, whole home repiping, sewer lateral work, gas line installation and most remodel plumbing all require one. Replacing a faucet or a toilet in place generally does not.

The permit exists so an inspector confirms the work meets code. That protects you, and it is the part of the job that matters most if something goes wrong years later.

What needs a permit

Permit required or not

  • Water heater replacement. Permit: Yes.
  • Whole home repipe. Permit: Yes.
  • Sewer lateral repair or replacement. Permit: Yes.
  • Gas line installation or extension. Permit: Yes.
  • Moving a drain or changing venting. Permit: Yes.
  • Like for like faucet or toilet swap. Permit: Usually no.
  • Clearing a drain. Permit: No.

Water heaters are the one most often skipped and the one most often questioned later. Water heater permits covers that case in detail.

Who pulls it

A licensed plumber pulls the permit and meets the inspector, and that should be included in the quoted price. It is a fair question to ask before agreeing to the work.

Which office handles it depends on your address. Some cities in the county run their own permitting and others route through Palm Beach County, which affects timing more than cost.

Why it matters years later

  • Unpermitted work surfaces during a sale and can delay closing.
  • Insurers ask about it after water damage claims.
  • The next plumber may find work that was never signed off.
  • Some associations require proof of permitted work in a unit.

If you are planning a remodel, the sequencing matters as much as the paperwork. The bathroom remodel checklist covers when the inspection has to happen.

Two jobs account for most residential permits here, and one compliance item sits alongside them. Repiping needs a permit, and irrigation brings backflow testing with it.

What a permit actually buys you

A permit is not a fee for permission. It is the mechanism that puts an independent inspection between the work and the finished wall. The value of it is entirely in the inspection, and the inspection looks at the things that are invisible once the job is closed up.

For plumbing that means the items with consequences rather than the items with appearances. Venting arrangements, which decide whether drains work and whether sewer gas stays out of the house. Pipe sizing, which decides whether fixtures perform. Pressure testing of new supply work. Correct materials and supports. Backflow protection where it is needed. Fixture clearances.

None of that affects whether the shower runs tomorrow. All of it affects whether the installation is sound in ten years, and whether a buyer's inspector finds something expensive.

What tends not to need one

  • Replacing a faucet, a showerhead or an aerator.
  • Replacing a toilet in the same position with no change to the drain or the flange.
  • Replacing a garbage disposal.
  • Clearing a drain, including cabling and jetting.
  • Replacing an angle stop or a supply line.
  • Repairing a leak by replacing a section of existing pipe in the same location.
  • Changing a shower cartridge or trim.

The dividing line is roughly between repair and replacement in kind on one side, and alteration of the system on the other. Moving a drain, adding a fixture, changing venting, repiping, or replacing a water heater generally crosses it. Because interpretations vary by jurisdiction, and this area spans more than one, the reliable step is a call to the building department for your specific address.

How unpermitted work surfaces later

Where it creates a problem

  • Selling the house. How it shows up: Permit history is public and routinely checked.
  • Insurance claim after a loss. How it shows up: Questions about whether work was inspected.
  • A later permitted project. How it shows up: Inspector sees the earlier work and may require correction.
  • Refinancing or an appraisal. How it shows up: Discrepancy between records and what is present.
  • A warranty claim. How it shows up: Manufacturer asks for evidence of proper installation.
  • A dispute with a contractor. How it shows up: No inspection record to establish what was done.

The selling case is the most common and the most expensive, because it arrives when you have least leverage. An open or missing permit discovered during a transaction becomes either a price reduction or a condition of closing, resolved under a deadline.

Getting it right from the start

  1. Ask whether the work needs a permit before agreeing a price, and get the answer in writing.
  2. Confirm the permit is included in the quoted price rather than billed later.
  3. Confirm it will be pulled under the license of the company doing the work.
  4. Ask who schedules the inspection and when it will happen relative to the work.
  5. Be available for the inspection, or arrange access, so it does not slip.
  6. Ask for written confirmation when the permit is closed out, not just when it is issued.
  7. Keep the documentation with your house records, alongside invoices and any photographs taken before the walls were closed.
  8. If you discover earlier unpermitted work, address it while you are not under a deadline.

That last point is the practical one. Retroactive permitting is usually possible and is far less painful when you are not three weeks from a closing date.

Frequently asked questions

Can a homeowner pull their own permit?

In many Florida jurisdictions yes, for their own residence. The work still has to pass inspection, and gas and venting are where self installs most often fail.

Does a permit slow the job down?

Rarely by much. Most residential work is inspected within a few days and the system stays in service in the meantime.

What if previous work was unpermitted?

It can usually be resolved after the fact. Deal with it before a sale rather than during one.

Can a homeowner pull their own permit?

In many jurisdictions a homeowner can pull a permit for work on their own residence, with conditions. It also means taking on responsibility for the work meeting requirements, and it removes the licensed contractor's accountability from the arrangement. For anything beyond simple work, having the contractor pull it under their own license is the stronger position for the homeowner.

Does a permit slow the job down?

In practice, rarely. Permits for routine residential plumbing are commonly issued quickly, and the inspection happens after the installation is working rather than before. A contractor who presents permitting as a delay is usually describing their own preference rather than a real constraint.

What if previous work was unpermitted?

It can usually be permitted retroactively, and a licensed contractor can handle the application and the inspection. There may be corrections required if something was genuinely done incorrectly, which is the point of the exercise. Dealing with it on your own schedule is considerably easier than during a sale.
