PEX, CPVC or Copper: Choosing Repipe Material

All three work here. They fail differently and they cost differently.

Choosing repipe material for a Boynton Beach home usually comes down to PEX, CPVC or copper. All three are code approved and all three will outlast the pipe you are replacing. The right choice depends on routing, budget and how the house is built.

The decision matters less than the workmanship, which is worth saying plainly. A well installed CPVC system will outperform a badly installed copper one every time.

How they compare

Repipe materials side by side

  • PEX. Strengths: Flexible, fewer joints, fast to install, tolerates movement. Watch for: Needs protection from direct sunlight.
  • CPVC. Strengths: Rigid, inexpensive, long record in Florida. Watch for: Becomes brittle with age, less forgiving of impact.
  • Copper. Strengths: Long life, rigid, familiar to every plumber. Watch for: Highest cost, vulnerable to pinholes in some water.

If pinholes are why you are repiping in the first place, putting the same copper back deserves a conversation. Why copper develops pinholes is the context for that.

Attic heat is the local factor

A Boynton Beach attic gets very hot in summer. That matters because most repipes here route through the attic, and heat accelerates aging in plastics.

PEX handles thermal movement well because it flexes. CPVC is rigid and needs correct support and expansion allowance. Either is fine when installed properly for the conditions.

Cost against lifespan

CPVC is usually the lowest material cost and PEX is close. Copper costs significantly more in both material and labor because every joint is made by hand.

Over a thirty year horizon the gap narrows, because labor dominates the total. The material choice is rarely what makes a repipe expensive. Access does. What a repipe involves explains why.

Material choice usually follows from what you are replacing. Polybutylene and galvanized pipe both lead to the same conversation.

How each material is joined, and why that matters

Joining method decides more about the outcome of a repipe than the material itself, because joints are where systems fail. Copper is soldered, which requires an open flame in your walls and attic and a clean, dry joint. Done well it lasts decades. Done poorly, with inadequate cleaning or flux left inside, it seeds corrosion at the joint.

CPVC is solvent welded. The cement chemically fuses the pipe and fitting into one piece, which is strong and reliable, but it needs correct dwell times and clean surfaces, and it cannot be done on a wet pipe. It also means no flame, which matters in a finished house.

PEX is joined mechanically, with crimp rings, press fittings or expansion fittings depending on the system. No flame, no solvent, and far fewer joints overall, because PEX runs continuously around corners where rigid pipe needs an elbow at every turn. Fewer joints is the single biggest reliability advantage in a retrofit.

What a repipe in a Florida house actually involves

In a slab-on-grade home the new pipe does not go back under the concrete. It is routed overhead through the attic and dropped down inside walls to each fixture. That approach is standard here and it is the reason a repipe is possible at all without breaking floors.

That routing is also why attic conditions matter so much. An attic in this climate reaches high temperatures for months at a time, and whatever material goes up there lives in that environment for its service life. All three materials are rated for it, but their behavior differs. PEX is flexible and tolerates thermal movement well. CPVC becomes more brittle with age and heat, and is less forgiving of a careless knock during later attic work. Copper handles heat without issue.

Insulation on the hot lines in the attic is worth specifying regardless of material. It shortens the wait for hot water, reduces standby loss and limits condensation on cold lines in humid air. It is a small line item that is frequently omitted.

Comparing the practical details

Beyond the material argument

  • Joints in a typical repipe. PEX: Fewest. CPVC: Many. Copper: Many.
  • Flame in the house. PEX: No. CPVC: No. Copper: Yes.
  • Behavior in hot attics. PEX: Flexible, tolerant. CPVC: More brittle with age. Copper: Unaffected.
  • Rodent interest. PEX: Can be chewed. CPVC: Rarely. Copper: No.
  • Repair years later. PEX: Simple, parts common. CPVC: Simple, needs cure time. Copper: Needs a torch.
  • Feel at the fixture. PEX: Quieter flow. CPVC: Quiet. Copper: Can transmit noise.

Rodent damage is worth a sentence because it is raised often and overstated. It happens, and it happens in attics with an existing rodent problem. Dealing with the rodents is the answer, not choosing a plumbing material around them.

What to pin down in the quote

  • Which specific system and fitting type, not just the material. PEX-A with expansion fittings and PEX-B with crimp rings are different systems.
  • Whether hot lines in the attic are insulated, and to what thickness.
  • How many fixtures and how many drops, so the quotes are comparable.
  • Whether a manifold or a trunk and branch layout is proposed, and why.
  • Whether the old pipe is abandoned in place or removed, which affects both price and future attic work.
  • Who patches the drywall, who textures it and who paints. This is the most common gap between two quotes that look similar.
  • Whether new angle stops and supply lines at each fixture are included.
  • Permit, inspection and the pressure test, in writing.

Drywall is where repipe quotes diverge most. A plumbing-only price against a price that includes patching, texture and paint can differ substantially, and neither is wrong as long as you know which one you are reading.

Frequently asked questions

Which is best for Florida?

PEX is the most common choice in new residential work here, with CPVC close behind. Both perform well when installed to spec.

Does PEX affect water taste?

Properly installed and flushed, no. Any initial taste clears after the system is flushed at commissioning.

Can I mix materials?

Yes, with correct transition fittings. Many repipes tie new PEX into existing copper stubs at fixtures.

Which would you use in my house?

It depends on routing and access. Call our Boynton Beach plumbers on (833) 567-2788 for a look and a written price.

Does PEX affect the taste of the water?

Some people notice a taste from new PEX in the first weeks, and it fades as the system is used. Flushing the system thoroughly after installation shortens it considerably. If taste is a standing concern in your household, a point-of-use filter at the kitchen tap addresses it regardless of what the pipe is made of.

Is copper still worth the premium?

It is a genuinely excellent material with a long record, and in a house where the existing copper lasted fifty years without trouble there is a reasonable case for staying with it. Where copper has already failed from pitting or aggressive water, repiping in the same material without changing the conditions is harder to justify. The decision should follow why the old system failed.

Can the repipe be done in stages?

Yes, and it is a sensible approach on a larger house or a tighter budget. Doing one bathroom or the hot side first is a real option, provided the plumber plans the layout so later stages connect cleanly rather than requiring rework. Say at the quoting stage that you want it staged, so it is designed that way from the start.
