How to Fix a Toilet That Keeps Running

It is almost always one of two parts, and both are inexpensive.

A running toilet in a Boynton Beach home is the cheapest plumbing problem to fix and one of the most expensive to ignore. Water passes continuously from the tank into the bowl and straight down the drain, and it does it quietly enough that many owners only notice on the bill.

The cause is nearly always the flapper or the fill valve. Both are inexpensive parts and both are a same visit repair, which makes this the easiest win in the house.

Find out which part it is

  1. Take the tank lid off and watch a full flush and refill.
  2. If water keeps trickling into the bowl after the tank fills, suspect the flapper.
  3. If the fill valve keeps running or cycles on and off, suspect the valve.
  4. Add a few drops of food coloring to the tank and wait twenty minutes. Color in the bowl confirms the flapper leaks.
  5. Check the water level against the fill line marked inside the tank.

The coloring test is the one that settles it. It costs nothing and takes the guesswork out of which part to buy.

What each fix involves

Common causes and repairs

  • Worn flapper. Symptom: Trickle into the bowl, color test positive. Repair: Replace the flapper.
  • Fill valve failing. Symptom: Valve runs or cycles repeatedly. Repair: Replace the fill valve.
  • Water level too high. Symptom: Water spilling into the overflow tube. Repair: Adjust the float.
  • Chain too short or tangled. Symptom: Flapper never seats fully. Repair: Adjust or replace the chain.

If the water level sits above the overflow tube, the toilet will run forever no matter how good the flapper is. Adjusting the float is free.

When it is not the toilet

If the toilet is sound but your bill still climbed, the leak is elsewhere. Causes of a high water bill runs through the rest of the list.

Water appearing at the base of the bowl is a different problem entirely, and it is one worth acting on quickly. A rocking toilet and the flange seal covers it.

While you are under there, look at the shutoff. A seized angle stop turns this small job into a large one, and the meter test confirms whether the toilet was the only leak.

How the two valves work together

A toilet tank has two jobs and a part for each. The flush valve lets the tank empty into the bowl when you operate the handle, and the flapper or canister seal is what holds that water in between flushes. The fill valve refills the tank afterwards and shuts off when the float tells it the level is right.

A toilet that runs continuously has a failure in one of those two. If water is passing from the tank into the bowl, the flapper or seal is not sealing, the tank level falls, and the fill valve keeps topping it up. If the fill valve itself will not close, water keeps entering and the excess escapes down the overflow tube.

Telling them apart takes one look. Put a few drops of food coloring in the tank and wait twenty minutes without flushing. Color appearing in the bowl means the flapper is leaking. No color in the bowl, with water still running, points at the fill valve or at a level set above the overflow.

Why flappers fail here faster than elsewhere

Flappers are rubber or silicone, and they fail from chemical attack and mineral deposits rather than from use. In this part of Florida both are in play. Mineral content in the water leaves deposits on the flapper seat, and a deposit only a fraction of a millimeter high is enough to break the seal.

In-tank cleaning products are the other common cause and they shorten flapper life dramatically. The tablets that sit in the tank release chemicals continuously onto the rubber parts, and a flapper that would have lasted years fails in months. The same applies to the fill valve seal.

Chlorine in treated water does the same thing more slowly. None of this is avoidable entirely, but it explains why a flapper is a consumable item rather than a permanent part, and why replacing one every few years is maintenance rather than a repair.

Doing the repair properly

  1. Close the angle stop behind the toilet, slowly. If it will not close or weeps around the stem, stop and replace the stop before anything else.
  2. Flush to empty the tank, then sponge out the remaining water so you can see what you are working with.
  3. Clean the flapper seat with a non-abrasive pad. A new flapper on a deposit-covered seat will leak just like the old one.
  4. Match the replacement to the toilet. Flapper sizes differ, canister systems are not flappers, and a near-enough part is the usual reason a repair fails.
  5. Set the water level to the mark inside the tank, roughly an inch below the top of the overflow tube.
  6. Check the refill tube is clipped above the water line and discharging into the overflow, not submerged.
  7. Open the stop, let it fill, and watch for a full cycle. Then dye test again to confirm the seal.

If the tank bolts or the tank-to-bowl gasket are weeping, deal with those at the same time. Removing a tank twice is the avoidable part of this job.

When the toilet is not worth repairing

Repair or replace

  • Flapper or fill valve, sound toilet. Usual answer: Repair, inexpensive parts.
  • Hairline crack in the tank or bowl. Usual answer: Replace, cracks do not stop spreading.
  • Repeated clogs with a weak flush. Usual answer: Replace, early low-flow designs flushed poorly.
  • Mineral buildup in the rim channel. Usual answer: Replace if cleaning does not restore the flush.
  • Rocking on the floor. Usual answer: Flange and seal repair first, then decide.
  • Obsolete parts, nothing matches. Usual answer: Replace, part hunting costs more than the fixture.

The weak-flush case is worth knowing about. Some first-generation low-flow toilets flushed poorly and no amount of repair changes that, because the bowl geometry is the problem. A modern fixture at the same water use flushes considerably better.

Frequently asked questions

How much water does a running toilet waste?

Enough to be obvious on a monthly bill. It runs continuously, so even a small flow adds up quickly.

Can I fix it myself?

Usually yes. Flappers and fill valves are inexpensive and designed for homeowner replacement. Shut the stop valve first and have a towel ready.

Why does it only run sometimes?

Often a chain catching under the flapper, or a flapper that seats on some flushes and not others. Replace it rather than adjusting repeatedly.

When should I call someone?

If the stop valve will not close, or water is at the base. Call Plumbers Boynton Beach on (833) 567-2788 or see toilet repair.

Can a running toilet raise my water bill a lot?

Yes, substantially, and it is the most common cause of an unexplained bill. The leak runs continuously, day and night, and because the water goes down the drain rather than onto the floor there is nothing to see. The dye test on every toilet in the house takes a few minutes and is the first thing to do when a bill jumps.

Why does it only run sometimes?

Usually a flapper that seals most of the time and occasionally catches on the chain, the refill tube or its own hinge. It can also be a fill valve that sticks intermittently. The intermittent version wastes just as much water over a month as a constant leak, and it is harder to notice, which makes it worse rather than better.

Should I replace both the flapper and the fill valve?

If the toilet is more than a few years old and you have the tank open anyway, yes. Both are inexpensive, both are consumables exposed to the same water, and the labor is the same visit. Replacing one and coming back for the other in six months is the more expensive path.
