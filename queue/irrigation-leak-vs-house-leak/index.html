Is the Leak in the House or the Irrigation?

One valve tells you, and it costs nothing to check.

Telling an irrigation leak from a house leak in Boynton Beach matters because the repairs are completely different. Both show up the same way on a water bill, and both move the meter when nothing inside is running.

The good news is that separating them takes one extra step. Isolate the irrigation at its valve and repeat the meter test. Whichever result changes tells you where the water is going.

The test

  1. Turn off every fixture and appliance inside the house.
  2. Record the meter reading, including the small dial.
  3. Wait two hours using no water, then read it again. Movement means a leak somewhere.
  4. Now close the irrigation isolation valve and repeat the two hour test.
  5. If the meter stops moving, the leak is in the irrigation. If it still moves, it is in the house.

The full meter test covers the first part in more detail, including what to do if your meter has no sweep dial.

Signs it is the irrigation

  • A patch of lawn that stays soft or unusually green.
  • A zone that seems to run longer than it is programmed to.
  • Water surfacing near a valve box after a cycle.
  • A bill that climbed when the irrigation schedule changed.

Irrigation leaks are common on larger lots, and the repair is usually straightforward because the pipe is shallow and accessible.

Signs it is the house

  • A warm patch on the floor, which points at a hot line under the slab.
  • The sound of running water with everything closed.
  • Damp baseboards or flooring that lifts.
  • A musty smell that returns after cleaning.

Those point at a slab leak. Slab leak signs and how hidden leaks are located cover what happens next.

How irrigation is plumbed, and why it matters

In most homes here the irrigation system taps off the main supply shortly after it enters the property, upstream of the house. It has its own isolation valve, a backflow prevention device, and from there a network of buried lines, zone valves and heads across the yard.

That arrangement is the reason irrigation is so often the answer to a mystery leak. Every part of it is buried, which means a failure produces no visible sign in a yard that is already irrigated. And because it sits downstream of the meter, every gallon it loses is billed.

Some properties in the western communities irrigate from a well or from a reclaimed supply instead, which changes the picture entirely. A well-fed system loses water that is never metered, so it will not show on a bill at all, and the symptom is a pump that runs when nothing is calling for water.

The isolation test done properly

  1. Note the meter reading, with the leak indicator position, and make sure nothing in the house is drawing water.
  2. Confirm the irrigation controller is off, not just between cycles. Unplug it if you are unsure.
  3. Find and close the irrigation isolation valve. It is usually in a valve box near where the service enters the property, often close to the backflow device.
  4. Wait thirty minutes and read the meter again. No movement means the loss was on the irrigation side.
  5. If it still moves, reopen the irrigation valve and close the main valve at the house instead. Movement now means the leak is in the service line between the meter and the building.
  6. If closing the house valve stops it, the leak is inside the house, and the fixture-by-fixture process applies.
  7. Record each result. Three readings taken this way narrow the problem to one of three distinct repairs.

This sequence is the highest-value thirty minutes available to a homeowner with a high bill, because it separates three completely different jobs: a yard irrigation repair, a service line repair and an interior repair.

Reading the yard

What the ground tells you

  • One patch greener than the rest. Points to: Leaking lateral or a weeping valve nearby.
  • Soft or spongy ground away from heads. Points to: Buried line leak.
  • Water pooling at a valve box. Points to: Zone valve not closing or a fitting failure.
  • Continuous dripping at the backflow device. Points to: Failed device, common and billed.
  • A head that seeps between cycles. Points to: Failed seal or a valve passing.
  • Erosion or sinking near the service line. Points to: Service line leak, not irrigation.
  • Mulch or soil washed away in one spot. Points to: Pressurized leak close to the surface.

The backflow device is worth checking first because it is above ground, it is a common failure point, and a device that drips continuously is easy to walk past for months. It is also the one item on that list a homeowner can inspect in two minutes.

Controllers and the other non-leak causes

Before concluding there is a leak at all, check the controller. A power outage can reset a controller to a factory default program, and that default is often longer and more frequent than anything you would set. A system that was running three times a week for ten minutes can come back running daily for thirty, and the bill reflects it exactly.

Rain sensors fail in the other direction. A sensor that has stopped working means the system waters through the wet season, which in this climate is a substantial volume for several months. The system is not leaking and it is still wasting water.

Zone valves that fail partly open are the in-between case. The system appears to run normally, and one zone also trickles continuously between cycles. The symptom is a consistently wet area and a bill that rose without a dramatic spike, which is why it gets attributed to a slow leak somewhere in the house.

Frequently asked questions

Where is the irrigation isolation valve?

Usually near the backflow device at the point of connection, often close to the meter. If you cannot find it, the backflow assembly is the landmark to look for.

Can a backflow device leak?

Yes. A discharging relief port on the assembly is a common cause of unexplained water use and it is visible once you know where to look.

Does the utility charge sewer on irrigation leaks?

That depends on whether you have a separate irrigation meter. Many providers bill sewer based on water use, which is why leaks out here can cost more than expected.

Where is the irrigation isolation valve?

Usually in a buried valve box within a few feet of where the water service enters the property, often beside or just downstream of the backflow device. Look for a rectangular lid at ground level, frequently overgrown. If you cannot find it, the backflow device itself normally has shutoffs on it, which serve the same purpose for this test.

Does the utility charge sewer on irrigation water?

It depends on how the property is metered. Some properties have a separate irrigation meter so that water is billed for water only, since it does not enter the sewer. Others run everything through one meter, in which case a large irrigation leak can be billed at both rates. Worth asking your utility, because the difference is significant.

Can a backflow device leak enough to matter?

Easily. A device that is weeping or discharging continuously loses water twenty-four hours a day, and because it is outside and often behind planting, nobody notices. Devices requiring annual testing exist partly for this reason. If yours is visibly wet underneath, that is a finding rather than a curiosity.
