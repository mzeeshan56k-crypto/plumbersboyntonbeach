What a Sewer Camera Inspection Actually Shows

Footage turns a guess into a decision. Here is how to read it.

A sewer camera inspection in Boynton Beach puts a recorded picture of your drain line on a screen. It shows where the pipe runs, how deep it sits and what is wrong with it. That footage is what decides between a spot repair, a liner and a full replacement.

Without it, every quote is an opinion. With it, you can see the crack, the offset joint or the belly for yourself, and the number attached to the repair stops being a mystery.

What the camera is looking for

  • Cracks and fractures, which let groundwater in and waste out.
  • Offset joints, where two sections have shifted apart.
  • Bellies, low spots where waste collects instead of flowing.
  • Root intrusion, usually at a joint and usually from a nearby tree.
  • Scale and build-up, which narrow the pipe from the inside.

It also records depth and distance. That tells the crew exactly where to dig if digging is needed, which keeps the hole small.

When it is worth paying for

Any line that has backed up more than once deserves a camera. So does any home you are about to buy that was built before 1980. Cast iron drain pipe in Florida homes explains why that age matters here.

It is also the honest answer to a quote you are unsure about. If a plumber recommends replacement without footage, ask to see the line first.

What happens after the footage

What the camera finds and what usually follows

  • Roots at one joint. Typical repair: Root cutting then a spot repair or liner. Scope: Small.
  • Single crack or offset. Typical repair: Spot repair at that point. Scope: Small to medium.
  • Belly along a run. Typical repair: Section replacement to restore fall. Scope: Medium.
  • Collapse or widespread cracking. Typical repair: Trenchless lining or full replacement. Scope: Large.

The repair is quoted from what the footage shows, not from a worst case. If the line is backing up right now, there are steps that limit the damage before anyone arrives.

How the inspection runs

  1. We find the access point. An exterior cleanout is ideal. Without one, we may pull a toilet or use a roof vent, which takes longer and costs more.
  2. The line gets a quick flush so the lens is looking at pipe rather than suspended waste.
  3. The camera head goes in on a flexible rod with a counter that measures how far it has traveled. That distance reading is what makes the footage actionable.
  4. We run to the connection point or to the obstruction, whichever comes first, noting every defect and its distance.
  5. A sonde in the camera head lets us mark the position of a defect on the ground above with a locator, so excavation goes to the right spot rather than a trench across the yard.

The distance counter and the locator are the parts that turn footage into a repair plan. Video of a cracked pipe is interesting. Video of a cracked pipe at thirty-one feet from the cleanout, marked with a flag on the lawn, is a quote.

What the footage cannot tell you

A camera shows the inside of the pipe. It does not show wall thickness, so a cast iron line that looks passable on screen may still be thin enough that jetting is a risk. Experience with the material and its age fills that gap, and it is why a good operator talks about pipe condition rather than just what is visible.

It also does not show the soil around the line. A pipe can be intact and still be the problem if the trench has settled and the fall is gone. What the camera gives you there is indirect evidence: standing water in a section that should drain means the pipe is holding liquid, and that is a pitch problem whatever the pipe wall looks like.

And it says nothing at all about supply plumbing. Drain lines and water lines are separate systems. A camera inspection will not find a slab leak, a pinhole in copper or a failing regulator. Those need different tools entirely.

When to pay for one before you have a problem

  • Before buying a home built before about 1980 in this area. A standard home inspection does not include a sewer camera, and the lateral is the single most expensive thing that can be wrong with a house of that age.
  • Before a bathroom or kitchen remodel. Opening the floor is the cheapest it will ever be to fix a drain defect, and finding it afterwards means doing the finishes twice.
  • Before approving any major drain repair quote. If someone wants to replace a run, the footage is how you confirm the whole run needs it.
  • After a repair, as a final check. The second run is what proves the work did what it was supposed to.
  • After roots have been cut once. Cutting buys time. The footage tells you how much time, and whether the joint they entered through is still open.

On a home purchase in particular, the inspection is cheap relative to what it rules out. Our sewer line work starts from footage in every case, including the quote.

Frequently asked questions

Do I get a copy of the footage?

Yes. The recording is yours, and it is worth keeping for insurance, for a future buyer and as a baseline if the line gives trouble again.

Can a camera go through a toilet?

It can, but we prefer an exterior cleanout where one exists. Pulling a toilet adds time and a new wax seal to the job.

Will the camera find a water leak?

No. A sewer camera looks inside drain lines. Pressurised water leaks are found with acoustic and thermal equipment instead, which the meter test helps narrow down first.

How long does an inspection take?

Usually under an hour for a residential lateral, including locating and marking. Call Plumbers Boynton Beach on (833) 567-2788 to book one.

Can you camera a line that is completely blocked?

Not past the blockage. The camera will reach the obstruction and show you what it is, which is often enough to decide the next step. To see the rest of the run, the line has to be opened first. On a full backup we usually clear, then camera, then decide, in that order.

Does the camera work in a two inch line?

Smaller push cameras do, with limits. Branch lines bend more tightly and the head can stall at a tee or a tight elbow. Main lines of three or four inches are where camera work is most useful and most reliable. If the problem is a single fixture branch, there are usually cheaper ways to diagnose it.

Will the footage hold up with an insurer or a seller?

It is far stronger evidence than a written opinion, especially with the distance counter visible and the defect located. Keep the file rather than a clip someone played for you on a phone. If you are using it in a negotiation or a claim, ask for the full run and not just the highlight, because the rest of the line is what establishes whether the defect is isolated.
