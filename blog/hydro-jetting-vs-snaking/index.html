Hydro Jetting vs Snaking: Which Does Your Line Need?

One punches a hole through the blockage. The other restores the full pipe diameter.

Choosing between hydro jetting and snaking comes down to what is in the pipe. Snaking, also called cabling, pushes a steel cable through the blockage and breaks it up. Hydro jetting sends water through a nozzle at high pressure and scours the pipe wall clean.

Both have a place. Cabling is faster and cheaper on a single soft clog. Jetting costs more and does more, because it restores the full diameter instead of opening a channel through the middle of the build-up.

What snaking does well

A cable is the right tool when something is stuck and the pipe is otherwise sound. Hair in a shower branch. A toy in a toilet. A soft blockage in a kitchen line that has not been building for years.

It is quick, it is inexpensive and in many homes it is all the line needs. The limit is what it leaves behind. A cable opens a path through grease and scale. It does not remove the layer that caused the clog.

What jetting does well

Jetting is the answer when the pipe wall itself is coated. Grease in a long kitchen run. Scale in older cast iron. Fine roots that have worked through a joint.

Because it cleans back to the pipe wall, the line stays clear far longer. That is why we use it on main lines and on any drain that has been cabled more than once. If roots are the cause, roots in a Palm Beach County sewer line explains what happens next.

How to choose

Picking the right method

  • One slow fixture, first time. Better choice: Cabling. Why: Fast, cheap and usually enough.
  • Kitchen line that re-clogs. Better choice: Jetting. Why: Removes the grease layer, not just a channel.
  • Main line backing up. Better choice: Jetting with a camera. Why: Diameter matters and you need to see the cause.
  • Known cracked or collapsed pipe. Better choice: Neither on its own. Why: Repair first. Cleaning will not hold.

We camera the line before jetting on anything older. A cracked pipe needs repair rather than pressure. A camera inspection settles that question before money is spent.

What it costs you to guess

Repeat cabling on a line that needs jetting is the most common waste we see. Three visits in two years costs more than one proper clean, and the line is worse at the end of it.

Going the other way is rarer but it happens. Jetting a simple hair clog is money spent on capability you did not need. The camera is what keeps both mistakes off your invoice.

What the equipment actually does

A drain cable is a steel coil on a drum, driven by a motor, with a cutting head on the end. The head spins as it is fed into the line. When it reaches the blockage it chews a path through it, and the waste behind it flushes away. The cable follows the pipe, which means it can turn bends that a rigid tool cannot.

A jetter is a pump, a hose and a nozzle. The nozzle has rear-facing jets that both propel the hose up the pipe and scour the wall as they pass. Front-facing jets open the blockage itself. The water pressure does the cutting, and the volume of water carries the debris back down to the cleanout. The pipe comes out of it with a wall you could see your reflection in, on a sound line.

That difference in mechanism is the whole argument. One makes a hole. One resurfaces the pipe. Neither is better in the abstract, because the right answer depends on whether your problem is an obstruction or a coating.

Where each one fails

  • A cable fails on grease. It cuts a channel, the grease closes behind it, and you are back in four months. This is the single most common reason a kitchen line gets cabled three times in a year.
  • A cable fails on scale. It knocks off the loose material and leaves the hard layer bonded to the pipe. Diameter stays reduced.
  • A cable fails on hard roots. It will trim what it can reach and leave the mass that is growing through the joint.
  • A jetter fails on a solid object. A toy, a toothbrush or a cleaning cloth is better retrieved than blasted, and jetting it just moves it further down the line.
  • A jetter fails on a pipe that has already lost structure. Pressure finds the weak point. On a line with a crack or a thin section, jetting can turn a slow drain into an open hole under a slab.

The last point is the one that matters most in older Boynton Beach homes, and it is the reason we camera before jetting anything that predates PVC. Our drain cleaning visits include that look before pressure goes anywhere near the line.

What to expect on the day

  1. We locate and open the cleanout. On homes without one, we discuss adding one, because every future visit is cheaper and less invasive with proper access.
  2. We run the camera first on any line that is older, has been cabled repeatedly, or backs up without an obvious cause.
  3. We pick the method based on what the footage shows and tell you which one and why before starting.
  4. We clear the line, then run the camera again on the same pass so you can see the difference rather than take our word for it.
  5. We flush the line with volume, usually by filling and releasing a tub, to confirm it holds up under real use rather than a trickle.

A straightforward cabling is usually under an hour. A jetting with camera work before and after typically runs two to three hours on a main line. Access, the length of the run and how much material has to come out are what move that number.

Frequently asked questions

Does hydro jetting damage pipes?

Not when the pipe is sound and the pressure is matched to it. The risk is jetting a line that is already cracked or badly corroded, which is why the camera comes first.

How long does jetting last?

On a kitchen line with normal use, several years. The point of jetting is that it removes the build-up, so there is nothing left for the next clog to form on.

Can I rent a drain snake and do it myself?

For a slow bathroom sink, often yes. For anything past the trap, a rented cable is easy to snap or jam in the line, and retrieving it costs more than the original job.

What pressure is used for hydro jetting a house drain?

Residential work does not need the pressures used on municipal mains. The nozzle design and the water volume matter more than the headline number, and a good operator matches both to the pipe material and its condition. The honest answer is that any plumber who quotes you a pressure before seeing the pipe is selling equipment rather than solving a problem.

Can jetting break a cast iron pipe?

It can, if the pipe has already lost wall thickness. Sound cast iron takes jetting well and benefits from it more than any other material, because scale is what closes it up. Thin or cracked cast iron needs repair, not cleaning. This is the entire reason the camera goes in first on any line of that age.

Is the mess worse with one than the other?

Jetting uses far more water, so containment matters. Done through an outside cleanout, almost nothing comes into the house. Done through an indoor access point because there is no cleanout, it needs care and drop cloths. If your home has no exterior cleanout, adding one is usually the cheapest single improvement you can make to future drain work.
