Why Your Water Bill Suddenly Doubled

Most of the time it is one of five things, and two of them are cheap.

A high water bill in Boynton Beach almost always has a physical cause. Water use does not jump on its own, so if nothing changed in the household, something is leaking. The job is working out which of a short list of causes applies to you.

Start with the cheap ones. A running toilet can waste thousands of gallons a month and costs very little to repair. Only once those are ruled out is it worth bringing in locating equipment.

The usual causes, cheapest first

Where the water is going

  • Running toilet. How to check: Food coloring in the tank, look for color in the bowl. Typical fix: Flapper or fill valve.
  • Irrigation leak or stuck valve. How to check: Isolate irrigation and repeat the meter test. Typical fix: Valve or line repair.
  • Dripping fixture or failed stop. How to check: Look under sinks for damp or corrosion. Typical fix: Washer, cartridge or angle stop.
  • Slab leak. How to check: Warm floor patch, sound of running water. Typical fix: Locate then spot repair or reroute.
  • Pool autofill. How to check: Isolate it and watch the meter. Typical fix: Float valve.

Run the meter test before anything else. It takes two hours and tells you whether you are chasing a leak at all.

The toilet is the first suspect

A flapper that does not seat lets water pass continuously and silently. Put a few drops of food coloring in the tank, wait twenty minutes and look in the bowl. Color in the bowl means the flapper is leaking.

This is a same visit repair and the cheapest item on this page. It is also the single most common answer to a bill that climbed without explanation.

When it is not a leak

Occasionally the cause is a meter or billing error, or a seasonal irrigation schedule nobody remembered changing. Compare the reading on your meter with the one on the bill before assuming a fault. If water is visibly escaping, close the main valve first.

If the meter is moving with everything closed, it is a leak. Slab leak signs cover the one that does the most damage if ignored.

Work out how much extra water you actually used

Before chasing a leak, quantify it. Your bill shows consumption in gallons or in thousands of gallons, and it usually shows the same period last year. Subtract the normal figure from the current one. That difference, divided by the number of days in the billing period, gives you gallons per day of unexplained use.

The number tells you where to look. A few hundred gallons a day is consistent with a running toilet or a seeping flapper. A thousand or more points at irrigation, a pool autofill or a supply line leak. A step change that appeared in one period and stayed is mechanical. A gradual climb over several periods is more often behavioral or a slowly worsening leak.

It also tells you whether to treat this as urgent. Water escaping under a slab or into the yard at a high rate is doing damage beyond the bill. The same volume going through an overflowing toilet into the sewer is only costing money.

The causes that are not leaks

  • Irrigation running more than you think. A controller reset after a power outage often reverts to a default schedule, and a zone that was set for ten minutes can come back at thirty, every day.
  • A pool autofill valve stuck open, or heavy evaporation and backwashing during a hot dry stretch.
  • A new water softener or filtration system regenerating far more often than it needs to. A unit set up on the wrong hardness figure can flush hundreds of gallons a week.
  • Guests, a returning family member, or a change in who is home during the day. Household size moves consumption more than most people expect.
  • A reverse osmosis system with a failing membrane, which can send several gallons to drain for every gallon it produces.
  • A rate change or a billing period of different length. Compare gallons rather than dollars before concluding anything.

Work through that list before paying anyone to look for a leak. Several of them are free to check and two of them are the most common answers we find.

Making the case for a leak adjustment

  1. Keep the bill that shows the spike and at least one normal bill for comparison.
  2. Document the meter test, with a photograph of the dial before and after if you can.
  3. Get the repair in writing, with a date, a description of what failed and what was done. An invoice that says service call is worth much less than one that identifies a failed supply line.
  4. Photograph the failure itself where possible, and keep the replaced part if it is small.
  5. Run the meter test again after the repair and keep that result too. Proof that consumption returned to normal is the strongest single piece of evidence.
  6. Ask the utility about its leak adjustment process early, because most have a time limit measured from the billing date rather than the repair date.

Palm Beach County Water Utilities serves much of this area, with the City of Boynton Beach serving parts of it, so check which one issues your bill before calling. Policies differ, and so does the paperwork each one wants.

Frequently asked questions

How much water can a running toilet waste?

Enough to show clearly on a monthly bill. It runs continuously, so a small flow adds up faster than most people expect.

Could it be the water heater?

Yes, if the relief valve is discharging or the tank is weeping. Look for water around the base and at the discharge pipe.

Does my utility help with leak adjustments?

Many providers offer a one time adjustment once a leak is repaired and documented. Keep the invoice and the written diagnosis.

Can a water softener cause a high bill?

Yes, and it is an under-recognized cause. A softener regenerates by flushing brine and rinsing the resin, and each cycle uses a meaningful volume. A unit set to regenerate on a timer rather than on actual usage, or programmed with the wrong hardness value, can run that cycle far more often than the household needs. Checking the settings costs nothing.

Should I ask the utility to test the meter?

It is a reasonable request when everything else has been ruled out, and most utilities will test on request. Meters do fail, though they more often under-register than over-register as they age. Do the house-side checks first, because a confirmed interior leak is a faster and more likely explanation than a faulty meter.

How quickly does a leak show up on a bill?

It depends where you are in the billing cycle. A leak that starts the day after a meter reading will show in full on the next bill. One that starts near the end of a cycle may show as a small rise, then a much larger one the following period. This is why two consecutive bills tell a clearer story than one, and why a mild increase is worth testing rather than ignoring.
